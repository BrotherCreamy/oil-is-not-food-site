Choose a better cooking fat

  • Butter for eggs, gentle cooking and finishing vegetables.
  • Ghee for pan-frying, sautéing and searing.
  • Beef tallow for frying chips or roasting potatoes.
  • Extra virgin olive oil for dressings, sautéing and roasting vegetables.

Health effects

What happens on your skin?

Vegetable oils may play an overlooked role in acne, inflamed skin, sunburn and body odour. This guide explains the concerns, where vegetable oils show up in everyday foods and what to cook with instead.

Acne

High consumption of vegetable oils can cause acne. On the other hand, consumption of butter and olive oil does not.

Body odour

Cooking oil can develop a rancid smell as it breaks down. This can cause foul body odour.

Inflamed, scaly skin

Flaky, inflamed patches around the nose can be caused by dietary vegetable oils. Many people also get the same flaky, red skin on the forehead, and on the sternum.

Bad sunburn

Sun exposure can oxidise fats in your skin. This can result in worse sunburn.

Linoleic acid, oxidation and frying

Linoleic acid is an essential omega-6 polyunsaturated fat found in many vegetable oils. During lipid peroxidation, reactive molecules attack fats and can produce compounds such as aldehydes. When these reactions overwhelm the body’s defences, they contribute to oxidative stress and can influence inflammation. Across controlled feeding trials, higher linoleic acid intake has not consistently increased common markers of inflammation.

Heat and time matter. Prolonged or repeated high-temperature frying breaks down oils and increases oxidation products. The rate varies with temperature, time, exposure to air and the oil’s composition. Avoid heating oil until it smokes and avoid repeatedly reusing frying oil.

At the shops

Check the label.

Vegetable oil turns up in foods you might never think of as oily. Check these aisles first.

  • Takeaway and eating outDeep-fried chips, fried chicken and meals cooked or finished in vegetable oil.
  • Snacks and baked goodsCrisps, crackers, commercial bread, pastries, cakes and biscuits.
  • Sauces and spreadsMayonnaise, ranch, creamy sauces, margarine, butter blends, oil-based spreads and dips.
  • Desserts and milky drinksIce cream and frozen desserts, coffee creamers and non-dairy cream substitutes.
  • Pantry mealsInstant noodles, including the seasoning sachets, and shelf-stable meal kits.

Spot the oil

Turn the pack around and read the ingredient list. Check the ingredients in brackets too.

  • Soybean
  • Canola
  • Sunflower
  • Corn
  • Cottonseed
  • Safflower
  • Grapeseed
  • Rice bran

Watch for “vegetable oil”, “vegetable fat” or a blend. Compare similar products for sodium and added sugar.

At the table

Make the swap.

Start with the food you eat most often. Build meals around vegetables, fruit and potatoes, with fish, eggs, beans or moderate portions of lean steak, beef or lamb.

Takeaway to tray bake

Swap deep-fried chips for oven-roasted potatoes and vegetables. Add fish, eggs or lean steak. When eating out, ask what the kitchen cooks with and whether grilled dishes or sauce on the side are available.

Bottled sauce to homemade

Whisk extra virgin olive oil with lemon or vinegar. For creamy dressing, stir herbs into plain yoghurt or make mayonnaise with olive oil and pasteurised eggs. Refrigerate homemade mayonnaise promptly.

Cream substitute to dairy

Use a splash of milk or real dairy cream in coffee. For dessert, look for traditional ice cream made with milk and cream, or have fruit with plain yoghurt.

Eating out

Avoid fried foods, stir-fries, fried rice and curries. Order whole foods like grilled steak and vegetables instead.
